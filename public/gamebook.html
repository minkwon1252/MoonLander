Tech Race: International Cooperation & Competition

A one-screen, facilitator-run simulation of technology policy, diplomacy and global competition for engineering students. Built for the Selene Program.

Format: One laptop, one projector, one shared screen. No logins, no apps to install, no Wi-Fi required. Four teams stand in front of their section of the big screen, argue their case, and the facilitator clicks the decision. ~2 hours, 8 rounds.

Educational purpose

This is not a game about countries winning a race. It is a game about the responsibility of engineers in an age of technological competition. Every card forces a real tradeoff: more progress often costs money, energy, welfare, or trust. Every decision a team makes on stage is simultaneously a political, economic, diplomatic, ethical, and social decision — and the class should feel that weight, argue about it, and sometimes change their minds mid-round.

The facilitated debrief at the end is where the real learning happens: which tradeoffs were unavoidable, which decisions the room disagreed about, and what a "successful" technology strategy actually means.

Setup

  1. Connect your laptop to the room's projector or large screen.
  2. Open public/index.html in a browser (double-click the file, or serve the public/ folder locally, or open the GitHub Pages URL — see the README). No internet connection is required once the page is open.
  3. The game opens on a guide screen first — a scrollable walkthrough of the rules for everyone in the room to read while the game is set up.
  4. Optionally click 🔎 Start Screen Guide for an 11-step interactive tour of the board: it dims the screen and spotlights one element at a time (top bar, event guide, team panels, stats, change indicators, roadmap, policy card, decisions, effect preview, facilitator controls, round summary), each with a short explanation in large type. Press Enter to advance, or use the Next / Back / Skip Guide buttons. It runs on a demo board and changes nothing in the real game.
  5. The setup screen follows: four team boxes, each with nine technology-direction buttons.
  6. Call each team's representative to the front. They pick their nation's technology direction live, in front of the room, and explain in one sentence why.
  7. Once all four teams have a direction, click Start Game.

Flow: Guide screen → (optional) screen tour → Goal selection → Main game (8 rounds) → Final debrief.

Teams

Four teams, each representing a real national or regional innovation system. Identities are drawn from real structural features (energy dependence, financial depth, manufacturing base, openness) rather than stereotypes.

Choosing a technology direction

Each team picks one of nine directions before the game starts: Artificial Intelligence, Space Technology, Biotechnology, Semiconductors, Energy Systems, Climate Technology, Quantum Technology, Robotics, Advanced Materials. The choice shapes which policy cards a team receives, which roadmap it builds, and how global situations affect it — so it should feel like choosing a real national strategy, not a cosmetic skin.

State variables & danger thresholds

Every team tracks eight stats, each 0–100, all starting near 50 with small national modifiers. Almost every card trades one off against another.

StatWhat it representsDanger threshold
💰 TreasuryPublic money available to spend. Everything ambitious costs it.below 20 → fiscal crisis
⚡ Energy / ComputePower and computing capacity. Modern research runs on both.below 20 → power / compute shortage
🏛️ Political SupportPolitical capital to push decisions through and survive them.below 25 → political instability
❤️ Public WelfareCitizens' material wellbeing and their trust in the programme.below 25 → social unrest
🔬 R&D CapacityYour national ability to do research — labs, people, institutions. This is what drives roadmap progress; it is not the roadmap itself.below 20 → research capacity collapse
🌐 International ReputationHow other nations regard you.below 25 → diplomatically isolated (cooperation stops working)
🛡️ Security / SovereigntyResilience to espionage, coercion and dependency on others.below 25 → sovereignty at risk
🌱 EnvironmentEnvironmental health of your growth path. Cheap now, expensive later.below 25 → environmental backlash

R&D Capacity is not Roadmap Progress

These are deliberately two different things, and confusing them is the most common mistake:

  • R&D Capacity (a stat, 0–100) — how much research your country is capable of doing.
  • Roadmap Progress (a separate 0–100% track) — how far your chosen national project has actually got. It drives the five-stage visual roadmap and it starts at 0% for every team, regardless of starting stats.

Each round your R&D Capacity is converted into roadmap progress — but only if the rest of the country is holding together. See below.

Global Trust — and why it exists

Global Trust (shown in the top bar, starting at 60) represents the world's overall willingness to cooperate. It is a shared value: there is one number for the whole room, not one per team. It is not decoration — it multiplies real numbers every round:

When Global Trust is…What actually changes
High (toward 100)Cooperation bonuses, joint research and shared standards pay out up to 1.6×. Global shocks are cushioned to about 0.8× damage, because the world coordinates its response.
Low (toward 0)Cooperation rewards shrink to about 0.4× — barely worth doing. Global shocks hit at 1.6×, and losing a resource conflict costs far more, because nobody helps you recover.

It rises when two or more teams choose cooperative options in the same round, and when teams cooperate during a resource conflict. It falls when several teams compete at once or scramble aggressively for a scarce resource. Early rounds therefore set how punishing the later rounds are for everybody — including the teams that caused the decline.

General pattern: openness tends to raise reputation and tech progress but lowers security; protection raises security but costs reputation; industrial policy raises tech progress but costs treasury; environmental protection costs short-term growth but protects welfare and the environment. There is no move that only helps.

Balance, blocking & regression

This is the heart of the game's difficulty. Each round your roadmap advances by roughly R&D Capacity ÷ 5 — but only if your country can support it. At the end of every round the game counts how many of your eight stats sit below their danger threshold:

Stats in dangerStatusEffect on Roadmap Progress
0🟢 StableFull advance.
1🟡 StrainedAdvance is halved.
2🟠 BlockedAdvance is zero — the roadmap freezes entirely.
3 or more🔴 RegressingThe roadmap goes backwards. Work already done is lost.

A team with an extremely wide spread between its best and worst stat loses further progress on top of that, even if nothing has crossed a threshold yet — lopsided development is penalised on its own.

Development upkeep. Building is not free: every point of roadmap progress burns treasury and energy. The faster you build, the harder your money and your grid are squeezed — and that squeeze is usually what eventually trips the thresholds above. Fast growth is possible, but only for a team that keeps funding, power, politics, welfare, security and the environment all healthy at once.

A team must recover its weak stats back above threshold before the roadmap can move forward again. Each team's live status is shown as a badge on its panel, and the round summary names exactly which stats blocked or reversed progress.

Technology roadmaps

Each direction has a five-stage visual roadmap that fills as Roadmap Progress rises (every 20% completes a stage). The board renders it live — a growing neural network for AI, a rocket assembling piece by piece for Space, a filling DNA strand for Biotech, a chip blueprint wiring up for Semiconductors, and so on — so progress (and regression) is something the room can literally watch happen.

Every team's roadmap starts at 0%. Nobody gets a head start on their chosen technology, however strong their national stats are.

Policy cards

Each round, every team receives one policy card — 152 in total across all directions. A card presents a situation, a question, and two options — left and right, Reigns-style. Each option affects 2–4 stats, but the card only previews the 1–2 most influential effects so the board stays readable (e.g. "Treasury −8, R&D +5" on the left, "Welfare +6" on the right) — the full effect applies once the choice is confirmed. There is no "correct" side: both cost something.

Cards depend on your technology and your stage

Your cards are chosen from your domain and the stage your roadmap has reached, so the questions evolve as your national project matures:

Stage bandRoadmapA semiconductor programme is asked about…
Early0–39%lithography access, cleanroom water, materials supply, talent
Mid40–59%yield secrecy, fab subsidies versus schools
Late60–100%export controls, monopoly pricing, global standards

Every one of the nine technology directions has its own early, mid and late dilemmas. Occasionally (about one card in four) a team draws instead from the pool of general cross-cutting cards that any country could face — brain drain, research fraud, university–industry conflict, public distrust of technology.

Many cards deliberately ask social rather than purely resource questions: privacy versus model performance, military funding versus research freedom, automation versus jobs, biosecurity versus open science, export control versus cooperation, climate protection versus industrial speed, who owns a space resource, and whether public welfare should ever lose to the national technology race.

The team's representative argues their case in front of the room; the facilitator can jot a one-line rationale, then clicks the chosen side. Effects apply immediately and animate on the board, and each affected stat bar shows a small "(+3)" / "(−5)" beside it — that indicator stays visible until the next decision changes that team's stats again, so the room can see exactly what the last choice did.

International & global events

The facilitator never chooses what happens next — at the start of every round, one click ("🌐 Reveal Round Event") randomly draws exactly one shared event from a single pool of 38 international/global events plus 12 resource conflicts. The event's own type decides what the round looks like:

TypeWhat it means
⚠️ Global shockBad news for everyone — a crisis, an attack, a collapse.
✨ Global boostA shared opportunity — a breakthrough, a new consortium.
🔀 Mixed eventSome strategies get stronger, others get riskier, depending on each team's chosen direction and current stats.
⚖️ Strategic condition changeShifts a stat or a threshold in a way that changes how safe or risky future choices are — the meaning of "stable" just moved.
⚔️ Resource conflictSee below — a different, non-card round type.

For the first four types, the event applies automatically to all four teams (often unevenly, depending on their chosen direction and current stats) before that round's cards are dealt, so every team's card decision is a response to the same shared backdrop.

The effect guide

Every event is announced with a short "What this means" line in the banner, shown before anyone decides. It is deliberately qualitative — it never gives exact numbers — but it tells the room who is exposed and which strategies just got riskier. Examples:

  • "Energy Shock — energy-heavy strategies become riskier this round. Energy-secure teams gain relative advantage."
  • "Export-Control Tension — cooperation becomes harder, while security-focused choices gain value."
  • "Public Distrust — welfare and political-support penalties become more dangerous this round."

Read it aloud before taking decisions; it is the main thing that lets students reason about a round instead of guessing.

Domain-agnostic events

Eleven of the events belong to no technology domain at all — global recession, energy shock, public distrust of technology, talent migration, supply-chain disruption, cyberattack, climate disaster, export-control tension, research fraud, standards disputes and open science. These land on all four teams regardless of what they chose to build, so what separates the teams is how they have been playing, not which direction they picked.

Simplified interaction effects replace free-form negotiation. After all four teams choose their card option for the round, the board checks the pattern of choices:

  • If two or more teams chose a cooperative / open option, all of them gain reputation and a little shared technology progress — unless a team's reputation is already below 20, in which case cooperation quietly fails for them.
  • If one team chose protection while two or more others cooperated, the protectionist team gains security but loses reputation.
  • If three or more teams chose competition, global trust drops, weakening cooperation bonuses in future rounds.
  • If one team chose secrecy while another chose openness in the same round, the secretive team gets a short-term tech boost but raises diplomatic tension; the open team is rewarded with a little extra reputation.

Resource conflicts & Rock-Paper-Scissors

When the revealed event is a resource conflict — oil, electricity, semiconductor fab capacity, rare earths, lithium, cobalt, high-end GPUs, launch windows, satellite spectrum, clean water, research talent, or advanced lithography equipment — that round has no cards. Instead, all four teams choose one stance:

StanceEffect
⚔️ Compete aggressivelyBig potential payoff, but if two or more teams compete for the same resource, it is resolved live with Rock-Paper-Scissors on stage. The facilitator enters the winner; the winner gets the full bonus, the losers take a penalty.
🤝 CooperateSmaller, safer gains — shared access instead of an exclusive claim.
🌱 ConserveProtects welfare and the environment at the cost of a smaller R&D gain.
🔀 DiversifyModerate gain by investing around the bottleneck instead of fighting over it.

If only one team competes, they get the aggressive payoff automatically — no RPS needed. This keeps the simulation realistic (nations really do fight over chokepoint resources) while staying fast and physical for a live room.

Round structure

  1. Facilitator clicks 🌐 Reveal Round Event — the game randomly picks one event and its type.
  2. (Shock / boost / mixed / condition-change round) The event is announced and applied to all teams; each team is dealt a card.
  3. Each team's representative comes to the front, explains their reasoning, and the facilitator clicks their choice (or presses the arrow keys with that team focused).
  4. Once all four teams have decided, the facilitator applies round effects — interaction bonuses/penalties and the balance check (see above) resolve automatically.
  5. (Resource-conflict round) All four teams pick a stance; competing teams settle disputes with Rock-Paper-Scissors; the facilitator enters the winner.
  6. A round summary appears: what each team chose, why, what changed, and whether anyone's roadmap was slowed, blocked, or pushed into regression.
  7. Repeat for 8 rounds, then the game moves to the final debrief automatically.

How to "win"

The final score is half Roadmap Progress and half national balance (the average of all eight stats). Both halves matter: a team that races ahead on a collapsing country scores badly, and so does a perfectly stable country that never actually built anything. You have to do both at once — which is the whole point.

The debrief also highlights the most cooperative, most aggressive, and most resilient teams, and surfaces the single biggest tradeoff and the most ethically difficult decision of the session — these are usually more interesting to discuss than the numeric ranking itself.

Running it as facilitator

  • Press F (or click "☰ Facilitator") to open the side panel: manual stat adjustment, save/load, round-length settings, text size, and log export. Gameplay continues while it is open; close it with its × button, Escape, or the same top-bar button.
  • Press 1–4 to focus a team, then ←/→ to resolve their card without touching the mouse.
  • The rationale box under each pending card is optional but recommended — it feeds the exported log and the debrief.
  • Everything lives in memory in the browser tab; use Save in the facilitator panel to checkpoint to this browser, and Export JSON/CSV at any time (not just at the end) to keep a permanent record.

Final reflection questions

  • Which decision are you least comfortable with, looking back? Would you choose differently now?
  • Did racing ahead on technology progress ever come at a cost you didn't notice until later?
  • When did cooperation pay off, and when did it leave a team exposed?
  • Who "won," and does the highest score actually reflect the best national strategy?
  • As an engineer — not a policymaker — where in this game did you have the most real influence?
