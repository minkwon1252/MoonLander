Tech Race: International Cooperation & Competition

A one-screen, facilitator-run simulation of technology policy, diplomacy and global competition for engineering students. Built for the Selene Program.

Format: One laptop, one projector, one shared screen. No logins, no apps to install, no Wi-Fi required. Four teams stand in front of their section of the big screen, argue their case, and the facilitator clicks the decision. ~2 hours, 5–6 rounds.

Educational purpose

This is not a game about countries winning a race. It is a game about the responsibility of engineers in an age of technological competition. Every card forces a real tradeoff: more progress often costs money, energy, welfare, or trust. Every decision a team makes on stage is simultaneously a political, economic, diplomatic, ethical, and social decision — and the class should feel that weight, argue about it, and sometimes change their minds mid-round.

The facilitated debrief at the end is where the real learning happens: which tradeoffs were unavoidable, which decisions the room disagreed about, and what a "successful" technology strategy actually means.

Setup

  1. Connect your laptop to the room's projector or large screen.
  2. Open public/index.html in a browser (double-click the file, or serve the public/ folder locally, or open the GitHub Pages URL — see the README). No internet connection is required once the page is open.
  3. The game opens on a guide screen first — a scrollable walkthrough of the rules for everyone in the room to read while the game is set up. Click Continue to Team Setup when ready.
  4. The setup screen follows: four team boxes, each with nine technology-direction buttons.
  5. Call each team's representative to the front. They pick their nation's technology direction live, in front of the room, and explain in one sentence why.
  6. Once all four teams have a direction, click Start Game.

Flow: Guide screen → Goal selection → Main game (rounds) → Final debrief.

Teams

Four teams, each representing a real national or regional innovation system. Identities are drawn from real structural features (energy dependence, financial depth, manufacturing base, openness) rather than stereotypes.

Choosing a technology direction

Each team picks one of nine directions before the game starts: Artificial Intelligence, Space Technology, Biotechnology, Semiconductors, Energy Systems, Climate Technology, Quantum Technology, Robotics, Advanced Materials. The choice shapes which policy cards a team receives, which roadmap it builds, and how global situations affect it — so it should feel like choosing a real national strategy, not a cosmetic skin.

State variables & danger thresholds

Every team tracks eight stats, each 0–100. Almost every card trades one off against another.

StatWhat it representsDanger threshold
💰 TreasuryPublic funds available to spendBelow 15 → fiscal pressure
⚡ EnergyPower / compute capacity availableBelow 15 → tech progress limited
🏛️ Political SupportDomestic political capitalBelow 20 → instability risk
❤️ Public WelfareCitizens' material wellbeing & trustBelow 20 → social unrest
📈 Technology ProgressAdvancement toward the chosen goal—
🌐 International ReputationStanding with other nationsBelow 20 → cooperation becomes ineffective
🛡️ Security / SovereigntyResilience to espionage, coercion, dependencyBelow 20 → espionage / cyber risk
🌱 SustainabilityEnvironmental health of the growth pathBelow 20 → environmental backlash

General pattern: openness tends to raise reputation and tech progress but lowers security; protection raises security but costs reputation; industrial policy raises tech progress but costs treasury; environmental protection costs short-term growth but protects welfare and sustainability. There is no move that only helps.

Balance, blocking & regression

Technology Progress does not just climb forever. At the end of every round, the game checks how many of the other seven stats are below their danger threshold for each team, and gates that team's progress accordingly:

Danger statsStatusEffect on Technology Progress
0🟢 StableGrows normally.
1🟡 StrainedAny progress gained this round is halved.
2🟠 BlockedAll progress gained this round is cancelled — the roadmap freezes.
3+🔴 RegressingTechnology Progress actively falls, worse the more stats have collapsed.

On top of that, a team with an extremely wide spread between its best and worst stat pays an additional penalty even if Technology Progress itself is high — racing ahead while the rest of the system falls apart is never free. A team must recover its weak stats back above threshold before the roadmap can move forward again. Each team's live status (Stable / Strained / Blocked / Regressing) is shown as a small badge on its panel, and a round's summary explains exactly which stats blocked or reversed progress.

Technology roadmaps

Each direction has a five-stage visual roadmap that fills in as a team's (balance-gated) Technology Progress stat rises (every 20 points completes a stage). The board renders it live — a growing neural network for AI, a rocket assembling piece by piece for Space, a filling DNA strand for Biotech, a chip blueprint wiring up for Semiconductors, and so on — so progress (and regression) is something the room can literally watch happen.

Policy cards

Each round, every team receives one policy card matched to their chosen direction (most rounds) or a general cross-cutting card (occasionally) — 98 cards in total across all directions. A card presents a situation, a question, and two options — left and right, Reigns-style. Each option quietly affects 2–4 stats, but the card only previews the 1–2 most influential effects so the board stays readable (e.g. "Treasury −8, Tech +5" on the left, "Welfare +6" on the right) — the full effect applies once the choice is confirmed. There is no "correct" side: both cost something.

The team's representative argues their case in front of the room; the facilitator can jot a one-line rationale, then clicks the chosen side. Effects apply immediately and animate on the board, and each affected stat bar shows a small "(+3)" / "(−5)" beside it — that indicator stays visible until the next decision changes that team's stats again, so the room can see exactly what the last choice did.

International & global events

The facilitator never chooses what happens next — at the start of every round, one click ("🌐 Reveal Round Event") randomly draws exactly one shared event from a single pool of 27 international/global events plus 12 resource conflicts. The event's own type decides what the round looks like:

TypeWhat it means
⚠️ Global shockBad news for everyone — a crisis, an attack, a collapse.
✨ Global boostA shared opportunity — a breakthrough, a new consortium.
🔀 Mixed eventSome strategies get stronger, others get riskier, depending on each team's chosen direction and current stats.
⚖️ Strategic condition changeShifts a stat or a threshold in a way that changes how safe or risky future choices are — the meaning of "stable" just moved.
⚔️ Resource conflictSee below — a different, non-card round type.

For the first four types, the event applies automatically to all four teams (often unevenly, depending on their chosen direction and current stats) before that round's cards are dealt, so every team's card decision is a response to the same shared backdrop.

Simplified interaction effects replace free-form negotiation. After all four teams choose their card option for the round, the board checks the pattern of choices:

  • If two or more teams chose a cooperative / open option, all of them gain reputation and a little shared technology progress — unless a team's reputation is already below 20, in which case cooperation quietly fails for them.
  • If one team chose protection while two or more others cooperated, the protectionist team gains security but loses reputation.
  • If three or more teams chose competition, global trust drops, weakening cooperation bonuses in future rounds.
  • If one team chose secrecy while another chose openness in the same round, the secretive team gets a short-term tech boost but raises diplomatic tension; the open team is rewarded with a little extra reputation.

Resource conflicts & Rock-Paper-Scissors

When the revealed event is a resource conflict — oil, electricity, semiconductor fab capacity, rare earths, lithium, cobalt, high-end GPUs, launch windows, satellite spectrum, clean water, research talent, or advanced lithography equipment — that round has no cards. Instead, all four teams choose one stance:

StanceEffect
⚔️ Compete aggressivelyBig potential payoff, but if two or more teams compete for the same resource, it is resolved live with Rock-Paper-Scissors on stage. The facilitator enters the winner; the winner gets the full bonus, the losers take a penalty.
🤝 CooperateSmaller, safer gains — shared access instead of an exclusive claim.
🌱 ConserveProtects welfare and sustainability at the cost of a smaller technology gain.
🔀 DiversifyModerate gain by investing around the bottleneck instead of fighting over it.

If only one team competes, they get the aggressive payoff automatically — no RPS needed. This keeps the simulation realistic (nations really do fight over chokepoint resources) while staying fast and physical for a live room.

Round structure

  1. Facilitator clicks 🌐 Reveal Round Event — the game randomly picks one event and its type.
  2. (Shock / boost / mixed / condition-change round) The event is announced and applied to all teams; each team is dealt a card.
  3. Each team's representative comes to the front, explains their reasoning, and the facilitator clicks their choice (or presses the arrow keys with that team focused).
  4. Once all four teams have decided, the facilitator applies round effects — interaction bonuses/penalties and the balance check (see above) resolve automatically.
  5. (Resource-conflict round) All four teams pick a stance; competing teams settle disputes with Rock-Paper-Scissors; the facilitator enters the winner.
  6. A round summary appears: what each team chose, why, what changed, and whether anyone's roadmap was slowed, blocked, or pushed into regression.
  7. Repeat for 5–6 rounds, then the game moves to the final debrief automatically.

How to "win"

The final ranking is a balanced score — the average of all eight stats, not just Technology Progress. A team that races ahead on tech while gutting welfare, security, or the environment will not top the board. The debrief also highlights the most cooperative, most aggressive, and most resilient teams, and surfaces the single biggest tradeoff and the most ethically difficult decision of the session — these are usually more interesting to discuss than the numeric ranking itself.

Running it as facilitator

  • Press F (or click "☰ Facilitator") to open the side panel: manual stat adjustment, save/load, round-length settings, and log export.
  • Press 1–4 to focus a team, then ←/→ to resolve their card without touching the mouse.
  • The rationale box under each pending card is optional but recommended — it feeds the exported log and the debrief.
  • Everything lives in memory in the browser tab; use Save in the facilitator panel to checkpoint to this browser, and Export JSON/CSV at any time (not just at the end) to keep a permanent record.

Final reflection questions

  • Which decision are you least comfortable with, looking back? Would you choose differently now?
  • Did racing ahead on technology progress ever come at a cost you didn't notice until later?
  • When did cooperation pay off, and when did it leave a team exposed?
  • Who "won," and does the highest score actually reflect the best national strategy?
  • As an engineer — not a policymaker — where in this game did you have the most real influence?
