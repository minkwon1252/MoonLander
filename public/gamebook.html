A serious decision-card simulation of technology policy, diplomacy and global competition, designed for facilitated conference play with five teams over roughly two hours.

1 · Purpose

Tech Race exists to make future engineers wrestle with the questions their careers will actually pose:

  • How should engineers act when technology becomes a geopolitical instrument?
  • How do we balance innovation, openness, national security, public welfare, energy and the environment?
  • When should nations cooperate, compete, protect, share, or restrict?
  • What happens when technical progress outruns laws, institutions and public understanding?

It is not a quiz with right answers. It is a structured argument generator: every card forces a tradeoff, and the debrief is where the real learning happens.

2 · Story

The world has entered an age of accelerating technological competition. Five innovation systems — each a university team representing a national or regional model — must steer themselves through it. They make domestic policy, negotiate (and sometimes betray) one another internationally, and weather global shocks that arrive without warning. No one can win by tech alone: a system that races ahead while gutting its welfare, poisoning its environment, or burning every alliance will collapse under its own success.

4 · Technology Domains

Before play, each team picks one domain to lead. The choice shapes its cards, opportunities and scoring all game.

Available: Artificial Intelligence, Space, Semiconductors, Energy Systems, Climate Tech, Quantum, Biotechnology, Robotics, Advanced Materials, Cybersecurity.

Each domain carries its own dilemmas — AI faces compute, data and surveillance; Space faces launch cost, lunar law and debris; Semiconductors face export controls and chokepoints; Energy faces the grid, nuclear and public acceptance, and so on. Global events also hit your domain specially: an energy team thrives in a fusion breakthrough; a semiconductor team is exposed to a supply-chain collapse.

5 · State Variables

Each system tracks eight variables (0–100, except Treasury which can go negative into debt):

Energy / ComputePowers AI/space/semis. Below 15 → research stalls (−tech progress).
Political SupportCapacity for bold policy. Below 20 → instability.
Public WelfareBelow 20 → protests (−political support each round).

Supply ChainResilience to shocks. Below 20 → progress slows.

Separately, a hidden trust matrix tracks how much each pair of teams trusts one another (starts at 50). Cooperation, treaties and kept promises raise it; coercion, broken deals and exposed espionage destroy it. Trust powers the diplomacy score and the live cooperation-network graph shown on the projector.

6 · The Round

  1. Domestic phase. Each team privately receives one policy card and chooses option A or B, recording a short rationale. Effects apply immediately.
  2. International phase. Each team may make one move toward another team (or all). Targets either accept or decline a proposal; a unilateral hit can be absorbed or retaliated against.
  3. Global phase. The facilitator reveals a world event that hits everyone — after choices are locked, so teams carry real uncertainty.
  4. Summary. Threshold penalties resolve, stats settle, and scores plus the cooperation-network graph update on the projector so the room can read each team's status before the next round.

Representatives come to the front during the international phase to explain their choices on the big screen; the facilitator records what they say. The facilitator can run a visible countdown timer for any phase and may end the game at any time — the projector then shows final results and how close each team came to its goal.

7 · International System

Actions fall into three families:

  • Cooperation (requires consent): research pacts, shared compute, joint missions, talent exchange, safety/standards, data sharing, grid & supply cooperation, alliances, treaties, tech transfer. Both sides gain if accepted; trust rises.
  • Coercion (unilateral): export restrictions, protection laws, accusations, breaking agreements. They hurt a rival now but cost you reputation and trust — and invite retaliation.
  • Covert (secret, risky): covert acquisition, aggressive poaching, cyber intrusion. They resolve immediately and secretly. They may leap your progress forward — or be exposed, devastating your reputation and trust.

A multilateral treaty is offered to every team at once; each decides individually. Treaties and alliances pay off most when a global crisis later strikes.

8 · Global Events

Sixteen plausible shocks — financial crises, AI and fusion breakthroughs, energy and supply shocks, climate and health disasters, cyberattacks, debris cascades, talent migrations, new treaties, public backlash, a quantum crypto break. Each hits all teams, hits your chosen domain specially, and applies conditional modifiers: the unprepared (low security, low environment, low supply chain, closed systems) suffer more; the resilient and cooperative gain. Earlier neglect comes due here.

9 · Scoring

Five sub-scores combine into a composite. The design deliberately punishes "win the tech number, lose everything else."

ScoreBuilt fromWeight
Tech LeadershipTech progress + domain-leader bonus25%
Social StabilityPublic welfare & political support20%
DiplomacyAverage trust, cooperation minus coercion & exposed espionage20%
Ethical EngineeringWelfare & sustainability, minus coercion & exposed espionage, minus "hollow" tech20%
ResilienceSupply chain, talent, energy15%

A collective Global Cooperation Index (average pairwise trust) measures whether the whole room handled the race constructively or destructively. A high individual score in a low-cooperation world is a warning, not just a victory.

Each team also has a goal: become the world leader in its domain while keeping society stable, sustainable, well-resourced and trusted. The debrief shows a per-team checklist and a 0–100% measure of how close each team got.

10 · Suggested 2-Hour Schedule

The game defaults to 10 rounds; the facilitator sets the count live (1–15) and can end the game at any time. Use the built-in phase timers to keep pace. With 10 rounds, run shorter rounds (~8 min); for richer front-of-room debate, set 5–6 rounds (~12–15 min each).

TimeActivity
0:00–0:10Introduction & rules
0:10–0:20Team setup & domain selection
0:20–1:40Rounds (≈8 min each for 10 rounds, or ~13 min each for 6): decide → present → reveal global → score
1:40–1:50Final round / final global event
1:50–2:00Results & reflection (debrief)

Keep each round moving — the discussion at the front is the point, not optimizing the numbers.

11 · Example Cards

Domestic — "Data Centers vs the Grid" (AI)

AI data centers now consume a tenth of national electricity. Utilities warn of brownouts; households face rising bills. Prioritize compute, or household electricity stability?

A: Prioritize AI compute → +Tech, −Energy, −Welfare. B: Cap data-center load → −Tech, +Energy, +Welfare.

International — "Covert Technology Acquisition"

Secretly attempt to acquire a rival's technology. Resolves immediately and secretly. ~55% to succeed (+9 Tech); ~50% to be discovered (heavy political-support loss and a collapse of trust).

Global — "Semiconductor Supply-Chain Collapse"

A chokepoint failure freezes advanced-chip supply worldwide. All teams: −supply chain, −tech. Semiconductor teams gain leverage; teams with already-weak supply chains suffer extra.

12 · Debrief Questions

  • Which policies created fast innovation but hidden social, environmental or trust costs?
  • Which teams benefited from cooperation — and who paid for isolation?
  • Did any team buy leadership by sacrificing welfare, energy stability or the planet?
  • Did secrecy protect innovation, or did it isolate and weaken the system?
  • Were you neutral engineers, national strategists, or political actors? When did those roles collide?
  • What should future engineers carry from this simulation into real careers?

Interpreting results: read the composite alongside the Global Cooperation Index. A polished individual score in a collapsing, low-trust world is exactly the failure mode the game is built to expose.
